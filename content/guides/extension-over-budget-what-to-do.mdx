You opened the latest invoice. It's worse than you thought. The number is higher than the entire budget you started with, and you're only two-thirds through the build.

This is the moment most homeowners panic. Some stop paying. Some start arguments. Some try to fire the builder mid-project. All of these reactions make things worse.

Here's what to do instead.

## Stop and Assess the Damage

Before you do anything else, you need to understand exactly where you are. Not approximately. Exactly.

<Callout type="important">
Get the full picture before taking any action. Emotional decisions made in the first 48 hours often cost more than the original overrun.
</Callout>

Pull together these numbers:
- Total paid to date (all invoices, all trades)
- Work genuinely completed (be honest)
- Original budget
- Original quoted price
- Current total expected cost

The gap between what you've paid and what work is complete tells you whether you're overpaying for completed work or whether the scope itself has expanded.

## Identify What Went Wrong

Extensions don't go over budget randomly. They go over budget for specific, predictable reasons.

<Stat value="94%" label="of overruns stem from just five root causes" />

The five primary causes:

1. **Specification gaps** — The quote didn't include items you assumed were included
2. **Design changes** — Decisions made during the build that weren't in the original scope
3. **Site conditions** — Discoveries during excavation or demolition
4. **Price escalation** — Materials or labour costing more than quoted
5. **Scope creep** — "While we're at it" additions that seemed small

Be brutally honest about which category your overrun falls into. If it's specification gaps or design changes, that's on you and your professional team. If it's site conditions, that should have been flagged as a risk. If it's price escalation without a fixed-price contract, that's a contract problem.

## Take Immediate Control

Once you understand the situation, implement these controls immediately:

### 1. Freeze All Variations

No more "while we're at it" decisions. Every single change request from this point forward goes through a formal variation process:
- Written description of the change
- Cost implication (quoted, not estimated)
- Your written approval before work starts

<ToolCTA tool="variation-register" />

### 2. Review Remaining Scope

Go through every item that hasn't been completed yet. For each one, ask:
- Is this essential for the building to function?
- Is this essential for Building Regulations sign-off?
- Can this be done later (after funds recover)?
- Can this be simplified without compromising quality?

You'd be surprised how much optional finish work can be deferred. Landscaping, internal decorating, fitted furniture, final floor finishes — these can all happen after completion if cashflow is critical.

### 3. Verify Payment Against Progress

Stop paying ahead of work. Many homeowners find they've paid 70% of the contract value for 50% of the completed work.

<Callout type="warning">
Never pay for work that hasn't been completed. Retention exists for a reason.
</Callout>

Compare your payment schedule against actual site progress. If you've overpaid relative to completion, you have leverage. If you've paid in line with completion, you need to focus on controlling remaining costs.

## The Difficult Conversations

You need to have direct conversations with your builder and your professional team (if you have one).

### With Your Builder

Don't approach this as adversarial. Present the numbers. Ask for their assessment of remaining costs. Get a fixed price for completion if possible.

Key questions:
- What is the realistic total to complete as currently specified?
- Where can scope be reduced to save money?
- What variations have been instructed verbally that aren't documented?
- Can remaining work be fixed-price?

### With Your Architect or Project Manager

If you're working with professionals, they should have been tracking this. Ask:
- Why wasn't I warned about this trajectory earlier?
- What financial controls are in place?
- What's your recommendation for completing within a revised budget?

## Rebuild Your Budget

With all this information, create a new budget that reflects reality. Include:

<ExampleBox title="Revised Budget Structure">
- **Sunk costs**: Everything paid to date (this is fixed, no point agonising)
- **Committed costs**: Work contracted but not yet invoiced
- **Remaining quoted work**: Original scope items not yet started
- **Known variations**: Approved changes not in original quote
- **Risk allowance**: 15% minimum on remaining work
- **Professional fees**: Outstanding architect/engineer/surveyor invoices
- **Statutory costs**: Building Control, party wall, any remaining planning fees
</ExampleBox>

This revised budget is your new baseline. Compare it against your available funds and financing capacity. If there's a shortfall, you need to address it through scope reduction, additional financing, or a combination.

## Preventing Future Overruns

For the remainder of the project, implement these controls:

### Weekly Cost Tracking

Every week, update your costs. Compare actual spend against revised budget. Catch deviations early when they're small.

### Variation Discipline

The variation register isn't optional. Every change, no matter how small, gets documented with a cost before approval.

### Payment Milestones

Align payments to completed milestones, not dates. Don't pay for the roof until the roof is complete. Don't pay for plastering until plastering is complete and inspected.

### Regular Site Meetings

Weekly site meetings with documented minutes. Decisions made verbally become variations without cost control. Everything in writing.

## When to Seek Help

Consider getting independent advice if:
- The overrun exceeds 50% of the original budget
- There's a dispute about what was included in the original quote
- You suspect you're being overcharged
- The builder is threatening to stop work
- Quality issues are emerging alongside cost issues

A quantity surveyor can provide an independent valuation of work completed. A construction lawyer can advise on contractual rights. These professionals cost money, but they can save far more if the situation is genuinely problematic.

## The Path Forward

Here's the hard truth: you can't unspend money. The best you can do now is complete the project at the minimum additional cost while maintaining quality and compliance.

Focus on:
1. Understanding exactly where you are (complete financial picture)
2. Controlling remaining costs (variations, scope, payment alignment)
3. Completing the essential work (defer optional items if needed)
4. Learning for next time (documentation, contracts, professional oversight)

Most extensions do get completed. Most homeowners do survive budget overruns. The ones who come through best are those who face the situation clearly, implement controls immediately, and make rational decisions rather than emotional ones.

Your extension can still be successful. It will cost more than you hoped. But with proper management from this point forward, you can limit the damage and end up with the space you wanted.
